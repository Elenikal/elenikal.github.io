Teaching economics in the AI era

AI has made producing a competent answer almost free. So I teach, and assess, what stays scarce: framing the right question, judging the evidence, knowing when a model is wrong, and defending a decision.

The premise

In economic terms, generative AI pushes the marginal cost of producing a problem set solution, a literature summary, a regression script or an essay close to zero. Once something is cheap, it stops being a credible signal of learning. My courses shift student time toward what remains scarce, while protecting the foundations it all rests on.

  • Asking the right questionFraming, the counterfactual, what would change your mind.
  • Judging evidenceIdentification, measurement, data provenance, plausibility.
  • Knowing when an AI is wrongThe bad assumption, the leaked future data, the invented citation.
  • Deciding and defendingMaking a call under uncertainty and standing behind it.

Three lanes

Every task in my syllabi carries one label, so students always know the rule. The lane decides how AI may be used, not how hard the task is.

Closed

Foundations, without AI

In the room, on paper or out loud. Students build the fluency they need to audit anything else.

Short in-class derivations, sampled oral checks, in-person exams.

Logged

Practice, with AI on the record

AI is allowed. Students submit what they asked, what they accepted or rejected, and how they checked it.

Problem sets, coding labs, study sessions with a course tutor.

Open

Judgment, over AI

AI use is expected. The task is to direct it, find its errors, and defend the result in person.

Error hunts, replications, policy briefs with a live defense.

Typical course weighting: 30% Closed · 25% Logged · 45% Open. First-year courses lean Closed; MSc courses lean Open.

Principles

  1. Protect the foundations.You cannot audit what you do not understand.
  2. Assess the process, not only the product.Reasoning and verification are what get graded.
  3. AI is a sparring partner, not an oracle.Every output is a claim to be checked.
  4. Make thinking visible out loud.Short, randomly sampled oral checks scale.
  5. Reward calibration.Students state probabilities and are scored on them.
  6. Teach AI as an economic object.Labor markets, productivity, and the measurement pitfalls of LLM-based research.
  7. Equal access by design.The university provides the tools; grades never depend on a paid subscription.

Module patterns

Nine reusable building blocks I combine into any economics course.

Closed

Oral Check

A sampled ten-minute viva on a student's own work: explain, perturb, extend.

The most AI-proof check there is.

Closed

Cold Derivation

A short handwritten derivation at the start of class. Frequent, low stakes.

e.g. the sign of omitted-variable bias.

Logged

Tutor Log

Study with a course tutor grounded in the notes, set to ask rather than answer.

Logs reveal the week's misconceptions.

Open

AI Duel

Grade an AI's answer to an economics question, then write a better one.

e.g. minimum wage and monopsony.

Open

Error Hunt

Audit an AI-written empirical paper with deliberately planted flaws.

Bad controls, wrong clustering, invented citations.

Open

Prediction Ledger

Probabilistic forecasts on real data releases, scored against human and LLM baselines.

Brier scores reward honest calibration.

Logged

Replication Sprint

Replicate a published result with an AI pair-programmer and document every check.

Graded on the verification memo.

Open

Brief Defense

A two-page policy brief, AI allowed, defended under live cross-examination.

Students own every sentence.

Open

Agent Economy

LLM agents play markets and games; compare them with theory and lab evidence.

When are silicon subjects a valid stand-in?

Teaching statement

My teaching starts from a simple economic observation. When the cost of producing a competent answer falls toward zero, the answer stops being evidence of learning. I therefore design courses around three lanes. In the closed lane, students derive and explain core results without AI. In the logged lane, they use AI for problem sets and coding but document what they asked, what they accepted and how they verified it. In the open lane, AI use is expected, and the task is to direct it, find its errors and defend the result. My research on text-as-data and LLM-based forecasting gives students a concrete reason to treat AI output as a claim to be tested, not a fact.
