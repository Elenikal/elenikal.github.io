Text as Data and Machine Learning for Finance and Economics

A ready-to-teach MSc module, designed for the age of large language models, on machine learning, text as data and LLMs for finance and macroeconomics. Students learn to build these tools, to measure with them, and to catch them when they are wrong.

Why this course

Finance and central banking now run on text and machine learning: earnings calls, filings, news and policy statements are read by models before they are read by people. Graduates need to build these tools, measure with them, and know when they fail. Most courses teach either the statistics or the tools. This one teaches both, and grades the judgment that connects them.

Who it is for

MSc students in finance, economics, business analytics or data science. Works as a core module or an elective, and as a PhD methods course with a research project in place of the Brief Defense.

Prerequisites

Introductory econometrics and some Python. Weeks 1 and 2 bring everyone to the same level on prediction and evaluation.

Formats

Ten weeks plus an exam as designed. It also condenses into a five-day intensive for executive education or a doctoral school: one block per day and a capstone on day five.

Teaching record

Built on seven years of teaching big data and text analytics to MSc students at King’s College London (2019–2026), redesigned from the ground up for the AI era. A complete sample week is available on request: slides, lab notebooks, an error-hunt exhibit with answer key, and a run-sheet.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the module, students can:

  1. Derive and explain the core estimators (ridge, lasso, principal components, attention) without notes.
  2. Build prediction and nowcasting pipelines that are honest about time: no leakage, point-in-time data, proper scoring.
  3. Turn financial and central bank text into measurements, and validate them against human judgment.
  4. Use language models as instruments, assistants and agents, and audit their output for errors, cost and bias.
  5. Detect look-ahead bias and training-data contamination in claims about AI forecasting.
  6. Defend a modelling decision in front of a risk committee, in the language of model risk and regulation.

Weekly schedule

Each week has a two-hour lecture and a one-hour lab, with one task in each lane. Lectures open with a ten-minute cold derivation.

Block I

Learning from high-dimensional data

Week1

Prediction when there are more variables than observations

When you have 120 macro series and 300 months, what can you learn, and what are you fooling yourself about?

  • Curse of dimensionality
  • Bias and variance
  • Ridge, lasso, elastic net
  • Cross-validation for time series
  • Leakage
Closed

Cold derivation: the ridge estimator in closed form, and why the lasso sets coefficients exactly to zero.

Logged

Lab: nowcast industrial production from FRED-MD with the lasso and rolling-origin validation. AI pair-programmer allowed; submit the log.

Open

AI Duel: an assistant reads the lasso output and claims “these 12 variables drive inflation”. Grade it: prediction is not causation, and selection is unstable.

Data FRED-MD monthly macro databaseReading Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 3; Tibshirani (1996), the lasso; McCracken and Ng (2016), FRED-MD
Week2

Nonlinear learners: trees, boosting and neural networks

Why does gradient boosting still win on tabular financial data, and when does a neural network earn its cost?

  • Trees and random forests
  • Gradient boosting
  • Feed-forward networks and backpropagation
  • Regularisation and early stopping
  • Statistical versus economic significance
Closed

Cold derivation: one backpropagation step by hand on a two-neuron network.

Logged

Lab: boosting versus a small network on stock-level return predictors, evaluated out of sample by date.

Open

Leakage Court (Error Hunt 1): a notebook claims 60% accuracy predicting Amazon’s next-day direction. Teams find the planted flaws: a random split on time series, scaling on the full sample, a feature that contains tomorrow.

Data Daily equity prices and volumes; stock characteristicsReading Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 10–11; Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020), empirical asset pricing via machine learning
Week3

Structure without labels: factors, clusters and autoencoders

Can a few hidden factors summarise the whole economy, and how would you know if they cannot?

  • Principal components
  • Approximate factor models and diffusion indexes
  • k-means and hierarchical clustering
  • Autoencoders
  • Dynamic factor models for nowcasting
Closed

Cold derivation: principal components of a 2×2 covariance matrix by hand.

Logged

Lab: a factor-based GDP nowcast with ragged-edge data; compare it with week 1’s lasso.

Open

Oral Check, sampled on the week 1–3 labs: explain one modelling choice, then say what breaks if it is wrong.

Data FRED-MD; quarterly GDP vintagesReading Stock and Watson (2002), diffusion indexes; Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 14
Block II

Text as data, from counts to language models

Week4

Text becomes numbers

What is lost, and what is invented, when a central bank statement becomes a row of counts?

  • Sourcing corpora: EDGAR, central bank texts, news licensing
  • Subword tokenisation (how language models see text)
  • Document-term matrices and tf-idf
  • Finance dictionaries versus general ones
Closed

Cold derivation: tf-idf weights for a three-document corpus by hand.

Logged

Lab: build a document-term matrix from FOMC statements and track Loughran–McDonald tone since 2000.

Open

AI Duel: ask an assistant to count words and tokens in a statement. Explain its errors with what you now know about tokenisation. Prediction Ledger opens this week.

Data FOMC statements; Loughran–McDonald dictionaryReading Gentzkow, Kelly and Taddy (2019), text as data; Loughran and McDonald (2011), finance dictionaries; Jurafsky and Martin, Speech and Language Processing, ch. 2
Week5

Meaning as geometry: embeddings and topics

If similar meanings sit close together in space, what does “close” mean for a risk disclosure?

  • word2vec
  • Contextual and sentence embeddings
  • Cosine similarity and semantic search
  • From LDA to embedding-based topics
Closed

Cold derivation: cosine similarity by hand, and why vector arithmetic on words sometimes works.

Logged

Lab: semantic search over 10-K risk-factor sections; LDA versus BERTopic on central bank speeches. Tutor Log sessions start.

Open

Error Hunt (short): an AI-written summary of “the main topics in the speeches”. Check every claim against the topic model you built.

Data SEC EDGAR 10-K filings; central bank speechesReading Mikolov et al. (2013), word2vec; Blei, Ng and Jordan (2003), LDA; Hansen, McMahon and Prat (2018), FOMC deliberation; Grootendorst (2022), BERTopic
Week6

Inside a language model

What is a large language model actually computing, and why does that make it confident when it is wrong?

  • Self-attention and the transformer block
  • Pretraining objectives: BERT versus GPT
  • Fine-tuning and instruction tuning
  • Context windows and knowledge cut-offs
  • Why models hallucinate
Closed

Cold derivation: self-attention for three tokens by hand, softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V.

Logged

Lab: fine-tune a small financial-sentiment classifier on Financial PhraseBank; compare it with the week 4 dictionary.

Open

Replication Sprint begins (pairs, three weeks): replicate a published text-as-data result with an AI pair-programmer, then extend it.

Data Financial PhraseBankReading Vaswani et al. (2017), attention; Devlin et al. (2019), BERT; Jurafsky and Martin, transformer chapters; Araci (2019), FinBERT
Block III

Language models as research and industry tools

Week7

Language models as measurement instruments

If a model labels 50,000 sentences as hawkish or dovish, is that a measurement, and how good is it?

  • Zero- and few-shot classification
  • Prompt sensitivity and structured outputs
  • Validation against human labels
  • Agreement statistics
  • Cost, reproducibility and version pinning
Closed

Cold derivation: precision, recall and Cohen’s kappa from a confusion matrix.

Logged

Measurement Bake-off: dictionary, fine-tuned classifier, open-weight model and a frontier model label FOMC sentences. Score each against 200 hand labels and plot accuracy against cost per 1,000 documents.

Open

Error Hunt 2: a paper builds an “LLM sentiment index” with no human validation and a prompt tuned on its test set. Find the flaws and propose the fix.

Data FOMC sentences with class hand labelsReading Gilardi, Alizadeh and Kubli (2023), LLMs for annotation; Ludwig, Mullainathan and Rambachan (2025), LLMs as an applied econometric framework; Korinek (2023), generative AI for economic research
Week8

Retrieval, agents and documents

Can an assistant answer questions about a bank’s annual report without inventing numbers?

  • Retrieval-augmented generation
  • Chunking, indexing and reranking
  • Faithfulness and citation checks
  • Tool use and agents
  • Prompt injection hidden in documents
Closed

Oral Check, sampled on the Bake-off: defend your validation design.

Logged

Lab: build a retrieval assistant over annual reports and score it on 30 questions with known answers.

Open

Agent Audit: an agent computes a leverage ratio from a 10-K. Trace every step, find where it went wrong, and write the test that would have caught it.

Data Bank annual reports and 10-K filingsReading Lewis et al. (2020), retrieval-augmented generation
Week9

Forecasting with text, and the look-ahead trap

If a model was trained on text from 2024, what does its “forecast” of 2022 really tell you?

  • Nowcasting with news and central bank text
  • Look-ahead bias and training-data contamination
  • Memorisation probes
  • Point-in-time evaluation
  • Density forecasts and scoring rules
Closed

Cold derivation: Brier and log scores by hand for three forecasts.

Logged

Lab: add a text index to the week 3 nowcast; probe a language model for memorised FOMC outcomes before and after its cut-off.

Open

Prediction Ledger review: where the class beat, and lost to, the consensus and a plain LLM baseline. Replication memo due.

Data News-based indices; FOMC decisions; model release datesReading Kalamara et al. (2022), making text count; Bybee et al. (2024), business news and business cycles; Lopez-Lira and Tang (2023), ChatGPT and stock returns; Glasserman and Lin (2023), look-ahead bias in GPT sentiment
Week10

Governance, risk and the economics of AI in finance

Would you sign off on this model if you were the bank’s model risk committee?

  • Model risk management (SR 11-7, PRA SS1/23)
  • The EU AI Act and high-risk uses such as credit scoring
  • Explainability and fairness
  • Data protection
  • Compute costs and labour-market effects
Closed

Exam preparation: practice questions under exam conditions.

Logged

Brief drafting with AI, logged.

Open

Brief Defense: a memo to a model risk committee on whether to approve an LLM-based credit early-warning system, defended in a 12-minute cross-examination.

Data Regulatory texts; your Bake-off and Agent Audit resultsReading Federal Reserve SR 11-7 (2011); PRA SS1/23 (2023); Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act; Eloundou et al. (2024), labour-market exposure to LLMs
Closed

Exam period: a 90-minute in-person written exam. Derive, interpret printed model output, and find the bugs in printed code.

Oral checks: after each project, a random 25% of students have a ten-minute viva on their own submission. The result moves the project grade up or down. Undisclosed AI use in a Logged or Open task is an integrity breach; disclosed use is never penalised. AI detectors are not used.

Signature projects

Week 2 · teams of 3

Leakage Court

A notebook claims 60% accuracy predicting Amazon’s next-day direction. Teams act as the prosecution and find the planted flaws. Points for each flaw proven, penalties for false accusations.

Week 7 · individual

Measurement Bake-off

Four ways to measure monetary policy tone, from a dictionary to a frontier model, scored against the class’s own hand labels. The output is an accuracy-against-cost frontier and a recommendation a research team could act on.

Weeks 4–10 · individual

Prediction Ledger

Every week students forecast real events with probabilities: central bank decisions, CPI prints, and the tone of the next FOMC statement. Brier scores are compared with the consensus and a plain language-model baseline.

Weeks 6–9 · pairs

Replication and Extension

Replicate a published text-as-data result with an AI pair-programmer, then extend it with a modern method. Graded on the verification memo, followed by an oral check.

Week 8 · teams of 3

Agent Audit

An agent computes a leverage ratio from a 10-K filing. Students trace its retrieval and arithmetic, find where it fails, and write the test that would have caught the error.

Week 10 · individual

Brief Defense

A two-page memo to a bank’s model risk committee on whether to approve an LLM-based early-warning system, defended under cross-examination with SR 11-7, PRA SS1/23 and the EU AI Act in hand.

Tools and AI policy

  • Stack: Python, pandas, scikit-learn, PyTorch, Hugging Face transformers and sentence-transformers. Versions are pinned in one course environment, so every result can be reproduced.
  • Models: a small open-weight language model on university compute, so students can inspect it and fix its version; and a university-licensed frontier assistant for every student. Nobody pays for a subscription to do well.
  • Compute: each student gets a fixed API and GPU budget. Spending it well is part of the Bake-off.
  • Data protection: course data is public or licensed. Student work goes only into tools covered by the university’s data agreements.
  • Lanes: every task on this page carries its lane. If a task is not labelled, ask before using AI.
